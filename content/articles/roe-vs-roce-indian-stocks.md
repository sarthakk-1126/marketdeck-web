## ROE and ROCE answer different questions

Return on equity (ROE) tells you how much profit a company earned relative to shareholders' equity. Return on capital employed (ROCE) asks how much operating profit the business generated from the broader capital employed in the business. For most non-financial companies, a beginner should read both. ROCE describes operating capital efficiency; ROE shows the profit produced relative to the accounting equity belonging to shareholders.

Neither ratio is automatically “better.” They use different profit measures and different denominators, so the first job is to check exactly what went into each number.

For this guide, we use these transparent conventions:

- **ROE = profit after tax ÷ average shareholders' equity × 100**
- **ROCE = EBIT ÷ average capital employed × 100**
- **Capital employed = total assets − current liabilities**

EBIT means earnings before interest and tax. “Average” means the simple average of the opening and closing balance for the period. Other providers can use different definitions, especially for capital employed, so record the formula before comparing companies.

> The useful beginner question is not “Which percentage is higher?” It is “What return does each percentage describe, and what made it change?”

## Start with the denominator

Shareholders' equity is the residual interest in a company after liabilities are deducted from assets. The IFRS Conceptual Framework defines equity as the residual interest in an entity's assets after deducting liabilities. [S3]

ROE therefore focuses on the equity base. If a company earns ₹120 crore of profit after tax on average equity of ₹600 crore, ROE is 20%.

ROCE looks wider. In our convention, capital employed is total assets minus current liabilities. It approximates the longer-term capital tied up in the business. If EBIT is ₹180 crore on average capital employed of ₹1,000 crore, ROCE is 18%.

That distinction matters because two companies can operate the same capital base with very different financing mixes. One may be mostly equity-funded; another may use more debt. ROE can move sharply when the equity base changes even if operating assets and operating profit do not change by the same amount.

NSE's financial-results system separates reporting periods and identifies whether results are standalone or consolidated. Those details can change the inputs used in a ratio, so verify the underlying filing rather than assuming two displayed ratios are directly comparable. [S2]

## Worked example: same ROCE, different ROE

Consider two hypothetical manufacturers. This is a teaching example, not a real company comparison.

Both businesses employ ₹1,000 crore of capital and generate ₹180 crore of EBIT. Both therefore have an 18% ROCE under our convention.

Company A has no interest-bearing debt. Company B funds ₹600 crore of the same capital base with debt carrying an assumed 8% annual interest cost. To keep the arithmetic simple, assume a 25% tax rate and no unusual income or expenses.

| Hypothetical item | Company A | Company B |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Capital employed | ₹1,000 cr | ₹1,000 cr |
| EBIT | ₹180 cr | ₹180 cr |
| ROCE | 18.0% | 18.0% |
| Debt | ₹0 | ₹600 cr |
| Equity | ₹1,000 cr | ₹400 cr |
| Interest expense | ₹0 | ₹48 cr |
| Profit before tax | ₹180 cr | ₹132 cr |
| Assumed tax | ₹45 cr | ₹33 cr |
| Profit after tax | ₹135 cr | ₹99 cr |
| ROE | 13.5% | 24.75% |

Company B shows the higher ROE even though both companies generated the same EBIT on the same capital employed. Debt replaced part of the equity denominator. Interest reduced Company B's profit after tax, but the equity base fell even more, so its ROE rose.

That does not make Company B automatically better or worse. It means the financing structure is different. If EBIT later falls to ₹100 crore while debt and interest costs stay unchanged, Company A has ₹100 crore before tax, while Company B has ₹52 crore before tax after the same ₹48 crore interest expense. The operating decline is identical, but fixed financing costs change what remains before tax.

The lesson is that ROE can be influenced by financing while ROCE keeps more attention on the return earned by the broader capital base.

## The ROE–ROCE gap is not a mechanical debt signal

A common shortcut says that if ROE is much higher than ROCE, debt must explain the whole gap. That is too simple.

ROE and ROCE differ in both numerator and denominator. ROE commonly uses profit after interest and tax; ROCE commonly uses EBIT before interest and tax. ROE uses shareholders' equity; ROCE uses a broader capital-employed measure. Tax rates, non-operating income, unusual items, cash balances and provider definitions can also affect the comparison.

So a large gap should trigger investigation, not a verdict. Check interest-bearing debt and finance costs, then inspect cash, unusual income or expenses, tax effects and changes in equity. If a provider's formula is unclear, recalculate the ratio from the filing using a convention you can repeat.

SEBI's investor due-diligence guidance recommends examining the cash-flow statement, income statement and balance sheet and comparing a company with competitors rather than relying on one headline metric. [S1]

## Why average balances matter

ROE and ROCE compare profit earned through a period with balance-sheet capital measured at a point in time. Using only the closing balance can create a timing mismatch when the denominator changed materially.

Suppose a hypothetical company begins the year with ₹500 crore of equity and ends with ₹700 crore. Average equity is ₹600 crore. If profit after tax is ₹120 crore:

- ROE using average equity = ₹120 ÷ ₹600 = **20%**
- ROE using closing equity = ₹120 ÷ ₹700 = **17.1%**

The company did not generate two different profits. The ratio changed because the denominator convention changed.

A simple average is still only an approximation. A large acquisition or share issue late in the year can make opening-and-closing averages imperfect. The important habit is to state the convention and investigate material changes.

The same issue applies to ROCE. A new plant can increase capital employed before it contributes a full year of EBIT. A temporary decline in ROCE may therefore reflect investment timing, weaker economics, or some combination of the two.

## There is no universal “good” percentage

Simple cut-offs such as 15% are common screening rules, but they are not universal standards.

A capital-light software company, a cement producer and a utility have different capital requirements and cycles. Compare a company with its own history and genuinely similar peers before interpreting the percentage.

For a beginner, a stronger sequence is to check the formula and reporting basis, review several years, compare similar businesses, ask how much new capital was required for growth, inspect cash generation, and understand debt and interest costs.

SEBI's due-diligence material points investors toward multiple years of financial statements and competitor comparison. [S1]

## When ROE needs extra care

A very small equity denominator can make ROE look unusually high. If equity is negative, a conventional ROE can become difficult to interpret.

A one-off gain can also lift profit after tax for one year. Reconcile an unusual jump in ROE with the notes to the financial statements.

Debt can change ROE by reducing the equity share of financing. The follow-up is to understand what the borrowing funded, what it costs and how the financing structure affects the company.

Cyclical earnings are another source of confusion. High margins near the top of a cycle can make return ratios look unusually strong. Several comparable periods provide more context than one peak year.

Finally, do not mix standalone profit with consolidated equity, or vice versa. NSE filings distinguish standalone from consolidated results. [S2] Keep numerator and denominator on the same reporting perimeter. ## When ROCE needs extra care

Capital employed can be defined in more than one way. If you mix definitions, you can create a ranking difference that is purely methodological.

A new factory can depress ROCE before production ramps up. An asset impairment can shrink the denominator and make a later percentage look better without an equivalent operational improvement. Large cash balances can also complicate comparisons between otherwise similar companies.

Financial companies need special care. For banks and many lenders, funding is central to the operating model rather than a simple financing layer attached to industrial assets. Do not force an industrial-company ROCE convention onto a bank and assume the result is comparable. Use sector-appropriate measures and regulated disclosures.

## A six-step checklist for Indian company analysis

### 1. Verify the period

Check whether profit and capital refer to the same annual or trailing period. Note major acquisitions, share issues or capex during that period.

### 2. Verify standalone versus consolidated

Use the reporting perimeter that matches the question and keep it consistent. The current SEBI listing framework and NSE filing system are primary places to verify listed-company financial-result documents and their status. [S2] [S4]

### 3. Write down the formula

Save the numerator, denominator and averaging convention. A ratio you cannot reproduce is difficult to audit.

### 4. Read the trend

A multi-year sequence can show whether returns are stable, improving, cyclical or falling after expansion. Explain the operational or accounting change behind a large move.

### 5. Explain the gap

Check debt, finance cost, tax, non-operating income, cash, acquisitions and equity changes. Do not treat the ROE–ROCE gap itself as a complete explanation.

## The beginner takeaway

ROE and ROCE are not competing scorecards. ROE asks what profit was earned relative to shareholder equity. ROCE asks what operating return was earned on the broader capital employed in the business.

If you remember one workflow, use this:

**ROCE → operating capital efficiency. ROE → shareholder-equity return. Gap → investigate. Trend → verify.**

## Frequently asked questions

### Is ROCE better than ROE?

Not universally. For many non-financial companies, ROCE is a useful measure of operating capital efficiency. ROE remains useful for understanding profit relative to shareholder equity.

### Can ROE be higher than ROCE?

Yes. A smaller equity base, financing, taxes, non-operating items and the different numerator definitions can all contribute. Treat the difference as a prompt for further analysis.

### What is a good ROE or ROCE in India?

There is no universal percentage across sectors and cycles. Compare the company with its own history and genuinely comparable peers using the same formula and reporting basis.

### Should I use average or closing equity?

For a full-period ROE, average opening and closing equity often matches period profit better than closing equity alone. A simple average can still be imperfect after a large mid-period transaction, so state the convention.

### Should I use ROCE for banks and NBFCs?

Be cautious. Funding is part of the operating model of banks and many lenders, so an industrial-company ROCE formula can be hard to interpret. Use sector-appropriate measures and regulated disclosures.
