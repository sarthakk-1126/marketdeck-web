## A stock screen is the beginning, not the verdict

A useful Indian stock-screening process starts with a business question, narrows a comparable universe, and checks the surviving companies against their filings. It does not turn a collection of attractive ratios into an automatic buy list. The purpose of this guide is to build a repeatable shortlist you can explain, including the reasons a company should be removed from it.

Suppose two manufacturers both show revenue growth of 15% and a return on capital employed of 20%. One collects cash promptly and finances expansion from operations. The other offers customers longer credit, accumulates inventory and borrows to fund the gap. A screen can make them look similar while the underlying businesses become less alike. That difference is where research begins.

The financial-statement distinction matters: the income statement describes performance over a period, the balance sheet shows financial position at a date, and the cash-flow statement explains movements in cash. They are connected, but they answer different questions. The SEC's educational guide provides a useful introduction to that relationship; it is a US educational source, not an Indian filing rulebook. ## Step 1: Define the universe before setting thresholds

Start by writing down which companies you can meaningfully compare. An Indian manufacturer, a bank and a software business can all report profit, but their assets, financing and working-capital models differ. A single debt-to-equity or operating-cash-flow threshold across all three can create false confidence.

For a first research exercise, choose a small set of non-financial companies with broadly similar operations. Record the exchange symbol, sector, business description, reporting currency and whether the figures are standalone or consolidated. Check that the displayed period is consistent. Do not compare one company's latest quarter with another company's full year merely because both numbers are labelled profit.

Then write the selection objective in plain language. For example: find established non-financial businesses whose operating performance is supported by cash generation, and whose capital requirements can be understood. That is a research objective. Find stocks guaranteed to double is not a testable screening specification.

Choose exclusions before looking at the winners. You might exclude records with missing statements, incomparable business models or unresolved restatements. Record these as data-quality decisions rather than implying the excluded companies are bad investments. Keeping an exclusion log prevents the universe from changing silently when a result becomes inconvenient.

## Step 2: Separate growth from the quality of growth

Revenue growth measures change, not value creation by itself. Ask what produced it: greater volume, higher selling prices, an acquisition, foreign-exchange translation, or a different reporting perimeter. An acquisition can increase consolidated sales without showing that the original business became stronger.

Use both absolute amounts and percentages. Growth from ₹20 crore to ₹30 crore is 50%; growth from ₹2,000 crore to ₹2,200 crore is 10%. These are very different operating problems. Neither percentage is automatically superior without considering the capital needed, the durability of demand and the starting point.

Build a three-column explanation for each company: what changed, what evidence supports that explanation, and what remains unknown. A management statement about stronger demand belongs in a different evidence column from a disclosed segment-sales figure. Both can be useful, but they should not be treated as equally verified measurements.

A sharp improvement in margin deserves the same discipline. Was it operating efficiency, a temporary raw-material benefit, a one-off gain, or a change in accounting presentation? Before annualising the latest quarter, compare it with the same quarter in the previous year and inspect the accompanying notes for unusual items.

## Step 3: Calculate ROCE with an explicit denominator

For this article, define return on capital employed as EBIT divided by average capital employed, multiplied by 100. Define capital employed as total assets minus current liabilities, and calculate the average from the opening and closing balances. This is our stated analytical convention, not a claim that every provider or company uses it.

In a hypothetical business, EBIT is ₹120 crore. Opening capital employed is ₹500 crore and closing capital employed is ₹700 crore. Average capital employed is ₹600 crore, giving ROCE of 20%. Using only the closing balance instead would produce approximately 17.1%. The business has not changed; the denominator has.

| --- | --- |
| EBIT | ₹120 crore |
| Opening capital employed | ₹500 crore |
| Closing capital employed | ₹700 crore |
| Average capital employed | ₹600 crore |
| ROCE on average capital employed | 20.0% |
| ROCE on closing capital employed | 17.1% |

Use a consistent definition before ranking peers. Also investigate exceptionally high ROCE. It may reflect a genuinely capital-efficient operation, but a low denominator, an asset sale or unusual liabilities can affect the result. A ratio that looks impressive is an invitation to inspect the components.

Finally, distinguish the return on the existing asset base from returns on new investment. A company can report attractive historical ROCE while its next expansion earns less. Ask how much capital growth requires and what evidence supports the expected return on that incremental capital.

## Step 4: Reconcile accounting profit with operating cash flow

Cash-flow statements classify cash movements into operating, investing and financing activities. IAS 7 describes this framework and explains that an indirect operating-cash-flow presentation adjusts profit for non-cash items and timing differences. These concepts help organise analysis; Indian companies must be read under their applicable reporting framework. [S2]

Here is a deliberately simplified example, not a company result or a statutory cash-flow template. Suppose profit after tax is ₹100 crore. Add ₹20 crore of depreciation. Receivables rise by ₹50 crore, inventory rises by ₹30 crore, and payables rise by ₹10 crore. Ignoring every other adjustment, the operating-cash proxy becomes ₹50 crore: 100 + 20 − 50 − 30 + 10.

The missing cash has not necessarily vanished. Some may be tied up in working capital. The question is whether that investment is temporary, recoverable and consistent with the business's growth. A distributor building inventory before a seasonal sales period is not the same case as a company struggling to collect overdue invoices.

Do not judge cash conversion from one period alone. Read several comparable periods, investigate seasonality and compare working-capital movements with sales. Equally, do not dismiss repeated cash shortfalls as temporary without checking whether previous explanations were eventually borne out.

A simple research ratio is cumulative operating cash flow divided by cumulative profit after tax over the same period. Use it as a prompt, not a universal pass mark. Negative or very small profits can make the ratio unstable, and differences in classification or business model can undermine peer comparisons. Keep the underlying amounts next to the ratio.

## Step 5: Ask what debt is financing

Debt deserves more analysis than a low-is-good label. Identify whether borrowing funds working capital, a new plant, an acquisition, refinancing or distributions. Then examine the maturity profile, the cash available to service it and any relevant disclosures about security or covenants.

Consider two hypothetical companies with identical debt of ₹200 crore. One holds ₹100 crore of unrestricted cash and produces stable operating cash. The other has ₹10 crore of cash, lumpy collections and a large repayment approaching. Gross debt alone misses the financing problem. Even net debt needs care when cash is restricted or unavailable to the borrowing entity.

Avoid treating a computed interest-coverage ratio as proof of safety. Historical EBIT can fall, interest expense can change, and cash timing matters. Build a small downside scenario: what happens to coverage if operating profit falls by one quarter? This is a sensitivity exercise, not a forecast or a lender's credit assessment.

For banks and other financial businesses, redesign this section around the relevant business and regulatory measures rather than importing a manufacturer's template. Comparability is a prerequisite for useful screening, not a minor adjustment after the ranking is complete.

## Step 6: Bring valuation back to the business

A good business and an attractive purchase price are different propositions. Price-to-earnings compares equity price with earnings per share under the definition you choose. Trailing, forward, standalone and consolidated earnings can yield different multiples. Label the basis before making a comparison.

For a hypothetical share priced at ₹300 with annual earnings per share of ₹15, P/E is 20 and earnings yield is 5%. If earnings fall to ₹10 with price unchanged, P/E rises to 30 and earnings yield falls to approximately 3.3%. A multiple is sensitive to both its numerator and its denominator.

Build a valuation range rather than defending a single precise number. Write a cautious, central and optimistic earnings scenario, explain what would have to happen in each, and apply a clearly stated range of valuation assumptions. The exercise is useful because it exposes dependencies; it does not establish fair value with certainty.

Be especially careful with cyclical earnings. An apparently low P/E at unusually high profits may tell a different story from a low P/E at depressed profits. Ask whether your denominator represents a repeatable level of earnings or an exceptional point in a cycle.

## Step 7: Turn the shortlist into a source-linked memo

For every surviving company, produce a one-page memo before adding more filters. Include the business model, three important measurements, the source and period for each, the main uncertainty, and evidence that would weaken your current view. A good memo should allow another reader to reproduce your starting facts.

NSE's announcement and financial-results pages are useful routes to issuer disclosures. Use the actual document, including its notes, rather than assuming a search result or data-provider summary contains the whole explanation. ## Common screening mistakes to catch before saving

The first is over-filtering. Adding thresholds until only two impressive historical winners remain can encode hindsight rather than a durable research process. Save the initial rules before seeing the shortlist, and record later changes with a reason.

The second is false precision. A difference between ROCE of 19.8% and 20.1% may be less important than an unresolved difference in calculation basis. Use reasonable bands and inspect the underlying business instead of treating the last decimal as a meaningful ranking signal.

The third is mixing timestamps. A current share price divided by old earnings is a specific trailing valuation measure, not a fully current picture of the business. Save the price date and filing period together. A research note should make it obvious what was known at the time it was written.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the best filters for fundamentally strong Indian stocks?

There is no universal set. Begin with a comparable universe, data completeness, business performance, cash conversion, capital efficiency and financing risk. Choose thresholds that match the research question and explain their limitations. A filter can identify candidates; it cannot certify investment quality.

### Is high ROCE enough to select a company?

No. Inspect the definition, denominator, history and reinvestment prospects. Pair it with cash generation, working-capital requirements, debt and valuation. A high historical percentage alone does not show what future growth will cost or what return an investor will earn.

### Should I use standalone or consolidated numbers?

Use the basis that matches the question and keep it consistent. Group-level research often requires consolidated information, while standalone statements may help isolate the listed parent's obligations or operations. Compare the scope and notes rather than treating either version as automatically superior.
