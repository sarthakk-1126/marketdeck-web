## Turn a reading session into a testable question

Finishing an investing book and knowing what to research next are different achievements. A memorable idea can help you notice something, but it does not supply the company evidence, current price or assumptions needed to evaluate it. This guide offers a repeatable bridge: choose one idea, translate it into a question, collect a small evidence set, perform a bounded exercise and record what would change your mind. The output is a research note you can revisit, not a buy list.

Start with the [Books & Investor Methods library](/screener/learning/books/). Its original guides explain selected themes and link to exercises. They do not reproduce complete books, and an exercise is a MarketDeck teaching application rather than an author's complete investing method. Read the referenced edition for the full argument. The workflow below is also MarketDeck's own synthesis. The examples use fictional companies and invented numbers; no live company has been evaluated or recommended.

## Choose the question before choosing the book

Suppose you are curious about a growing Indian manufacturer. You might ask whether its growth consumes cash, whether an apparently low multiple reflects temporary peak profits, or whether you are becoming too attached to your first impression. Those are three different questions. Trying to answer them with one favourite quote produces a weak note. Write one sentence beginning with “I want to understand whether…” and select a reading lens that helps investigate that sentence.

For behaviour and decision discipline, the [Psychology of Money guide](/screener/learning/books/psychology-money/) is a useful starting point. Housel's publisher describes a book about how people think about money through short stories; that is a different task from estimating a company's value. [S1] For a valuation uncertainty question, start with [margin of safety](/screener/learning/methods/margin-of-safety/). For a narrative that needs measurable assumptions, use [valuation story-to-numbers](/screener/learning/methods/valuation-story-to-numbers/). Damodaran's accompanying author materials explicitly connect business stories and valuation numbers. [S2]

These are entry points, not a ranking of authors. A research session can use more than one lens, but label when you switch. If the question changes halfway through, preserve the original question and explain why. That simple record makes it easier to distinguish new evidence from the temptation to find a more flattering framework.

## Make an idea observable

“Quality business” is too broad to investigate in a short session. “The company can grow without repeatedly raising outside capital” is narrower. It suggests looking at operating cash, investment needs, borrowing and share issuance over comparable periods. Even that statement needs definitions: does growth mean sales, units, operating profit or per-share cash generation? Does “outside capital” include customer advances? A useful question makes the denominator and time period visible.

Use a three-column note. In the first column write the idea in your own words. In the second record the observation that would support it. In the third describe an observation that would weaken it. Avoid copying book passages into the note. Your interpretation should be short enough that another reader can challenge it without having to guess your meaning.

| Working claim | Evidence to seek | Evidence that would weaken it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Growth funds itself | Operating cash covers recurring investment over several periods | Persistent borrowing while reported profit rises |
| Pricing power is durable | Stable volumes despite measured price increases | Volume loss, discounting or rising customer churn |
| A low multiple deserves investigation | Comparable earnings and a clear balance sheet | Peak-cycle margins, exceptional income or debt pressure |

These are proposed research prompts. None of the observations alone establishes business quality. A company can borrow rationally for expansion, and temporary working-capital movements can depress cash without destroying the business. The purpose of the table is to surface the next question.

## Build a small evidence packet

Open the [Research Terminal overview](/screener/research-terminal/) and then the available company workspace. Keep the company's identity, reporting basis, period and units together. For an Indian listed business, verify the underlying issuer filings and exchange disclosures. NSE's financial-results page provides a route to company and reporting-period searches. [S3] Use the actual filing, its notes and its publication date when drawing a conclusion; a table copied elsewhere may have dropped qualifications or mixed standalone and consolidated figures.

A compact packet might contain the annual report, the latest relevant result, one segment note and a dated price observation. Record where each item came from. If a figure is missing, leave it unavailable. Do not fill the gap with zero or silently replace it with a different reporting basis. Keep the reporting period distinct from the date the information became public, especially if you later turn the idea into a historical test.

Financial statements answer different questions. A balance sheet is a point-in-time position, while income and cash-flow statements describe activity across a period. The SEC's introductory statement guide explains these distinctions; it is an educational reference, not a statement of Indian reporting law. [S4] For this exercise, the practical lesson is to stop before combining a year-end stock with a quarterly flow without explaining the choice.

## Work a cash-conversion example

Consider fictional Aster Components. Its reported annual profit rises from INR 80 crore to INR 100 crore, while operating cash falls from INR 90 crore to INR 60 crore. Profit growth is (100 / 80) minus 1, or 25%. Operating cash divided by reported profit moves from 1.125 to 0.60. These calculations are descriptive checks, not a standardized quality score or an estimate of free cash flow.

The weak response is “cash conversion fell, reject the stock.” A better response is “what caused the difference?” Suppose the cash-flow reconciliation shows an additional INR 35 crore tied up in receivables and inventory. That gives you a concrete investigation: did the business prepare for contracted demand, extend more generous credit, suffer collection problems, or acquire a subsidiary? The same numerical movement can have different explanations.

Write a follow-up observation that would separate the explanations. You might compare collections and inventory after the next reporting period, examine receivable ageing, or check whether sales growth came from a new segment. Do not claim a causal explanation merely because management's narrative fits your preferred view. Keep management's statement, the accounting evidence and your inference in separate sentences.

Now change one assumption. If operating cash were INR 95 crore instead of INR 60 crore, the ratio would be 0.95. That would narrow the question but still not prove that all investment needs are funded. Capital expenditure, acquisitions and financing obligations remain outside that simple ratio. A worthwhile exercise teaches where its calculation stops.

## Use valuation as a range of assumptions

Take another fictional case. You estimate value at INR 150 per share and observe a price of INR 120. The difference relative to estimated value is (150 minus 120) / 150, or 20%. If your downside estimate is INR 110, the same price is about 9.1% above that estimate. Calling the first number a safety buffer does not make the underlying valuation reliable.

The [Intelligent Investor guide](/screener/learning/books/intelligent-investor/) and the margin exercise provide reading context. For the current research note, write what changed between INR 150 and INR 110: lower margins, a slower expansion path, more reinvestment, dilution or another stated assumption. This is MarketDeck's sensitivity exercise, not a claim that Graham prescribed these inputs or this modern spreadsheet process.

A useful note can end without a valuation verdict. “I cannot yet reconcile reinvestment with the growth story” is more informative than a precise value produced from unexplained assumptions. Use the [Little Book of Valuation guide](/screener/learning/books/little-valuation/) when you need a more focused explanation of the connection between the business and the model.

## Keep behaviour separate from arithmetic

A correctly calculated ratio does not tell you whether your decision process is disciplined. Before opening a new price chart, write what would make you pause the research and what evidence could reverse your view. After looking, record whether the question changed. This is an original reflection exercise, not a psychological diagnosis or a claim that one practice improves returns.

For example, you might notice that you accept a management explanation for your preferred company but demand independent evidence from its competitor. The immediate next step is to apply the same evidence requirement to both. You do not need to score yourself or publish the reflection. Personal notes should remain private; there is no SEO value in exposing a reader's notebook, holdings or financial circumstances.

## Finish with a reusable one-page note

Use six short blocks: question, reading lens, evidence, calculation, alternative explanation and next review trigger. The trigger should be an observable event such as a results release or a specific missing disclosure, rather than a promise to check the price every day. Link the exact book guide and the relevant research tool so that you can resume the same line of inquiry later.

A completed fictional note could read: “Aster's profit grew 25%, while operating cash fell to INR 60 crore. Receivables and inventory account for much of the difference. I have not established whether this is planned expansion or weaker collections. I will check the next comparable reporting period and ageing disclosures before updating the claim that growth funds itself.” This note is useful because its uncertainty is explicit.

Do not delete the old version when the evidence changes. Add a dated update that names the new observation and its effect on the claim. A research history should explain changing reasoning, not merely show the final answer with all the earlier uncertainty removed.

## What to do in your next reading session

Choose one guide, one fictional exercise and one company question. Spend the first session on definitions and evidence collection; spend the second on the calculation and its limitations. Use the [beginner reading path](/screener/learning/paths/beginner-investing-books/) if choosing the first book is the obstacle. If you already know the topic, go directly to its method guide instead of reading every related page.

The aim is not to force every investing book into a trading rule. Some ideas improve the questions you ask, some expose a modelling assumption, and others concern behaviour. A strategy that can be expressed as explicit historical rules needs a separate [backtest review process](/intelligence/notes/strategy-backtest-research-checklist/). A qualitative business judgement may remain a research memo. Preserve that distinction and the library becomes a working companion rather than a collection of inspirational summaries.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a book guide a complete summary?

No. MarketDeck guides select ideas and explain original exercises. Use the referenced book for the complete treatment, its qualifications and its edition-specific context.

### Must every idea become a screen?

No. A behavioural reflection or a question about customers may belong in a qualitative note. A screen is useful only when its inputs and limits fit the question.

### Where should I start if I have only twenty minutes?

Choose one question and collect one supporting observation and one possible counterexample. Save the uncertainty and the next evidence source; do not rush to a valuation verdict.
